SheTrades

A Trades Marketplace
Built for Women

Web App Marketplace B2C

SheTrades is a two-sided marketplace that gives women a safer, more trustworthy way to book home services, and gives female tradespeople a platform built around them instead of one retrofitted for them. Rather than treating trust as a policy footnote, the product treats it as the core interface problem to solve.

The platform helps users:

Search by trade View verified profiles Message & book safely Manage jobs in one place

The Problem

Many women don't feel comfortable having an unfamiliar man alone in their home for trade work.

This is especially true for women living alone, elderly women, and mothers home alone with children. At the same time, women working in the trades have far thinner referral networks than men, making it harder for them to find consistent work.

This creates a few problems:

Safety concerns booking a stranger Limited female-tradie visibility No dedicated platform to search on Trust is hard to verify online

The goal was to design a marketplace where trust isn't just implied, it's visible in the interface itself, from verified profiles to transparent reviews, so users feel safe from the first search.

The User

The primary users are women who want more control and comfort over who enters their home for trade work.

They may include:

  • Women living alone who prioritise safety over price
  • Elderly women who value trust and familiarity
  • Stay-at-home parents booking work while home with children
  • Female property investors and landlords managing multiple properties
  • Women's shelters coordinating maintenance and repair work

On the supply side, the same platform serves female tradespeople looking for a channel that doesn't rely on personal referrals to find consistent work.

Designing for both sides meant the interface had to build trust for the person booking, while still making it easy for the tradesperson to look credible and get discovered.

The Method

I approached this as translating a business strategy into an interface that people could actually trust and use.

Problem framing

I started from a Lean Canvas mapping the target users, value proposition, and unfair advantage, and translated it into user-facing design problems rather than business ones.

Persona mapping

I broke the customer segments down into concrete personas, from women living alone to elderly homeowners and property investors, to design for their specific comfort and trust needs.

Trust-first interface planning

I identified where trust needed to be visible in the UI: verified badges, reviews, identity checks, and job history, so safety wasn't just a claim.

Engagement mechanic design

I treated the "digital badge" social-proof idea from the strategy doc as a real design problem: how to turn a booking into something worth sharing.

Two-sided balancing

I weighed every decision against both sides of the marketplace: the person booking, and the tradesperson trying to be found.

The Process

I began by grounding the design in the two sides of the marketplace: the person booking a job, and the tradesperson completing it.

For the booking side, I focused on making trust visible immediately, search results needed to show verification status and reviews without extra taps. For the tradesperson side, I focused on a portfolio-style profile that could make their work credible at a glance.

One of the most interesting parts of the project was the social-proof mechanic.

The original strategy included a "digital badge", a shareable stat like "I've spent $4,200 supporting women in trades this year." I treated this as a genuine engagement and retention problem: how do you turn a completed booking into something a user wants to share, without it feeling like a marketing gimmick bolted onto the product.

I also thought through onboarding moments that use real user stories, like featuring tradeswomen who are underrepresented in their field, as a way to build trust and community feeling directly inside the product, not just in outside marketing.

For the visual direction, I paired the brand's coral as the primary accent with a soft blush background and dark, near-black text, keeping the product warm and human rather than clinical.

To keep the interface approachable rather than corporate, I focused on:

  • Strong contrast between coral accents and dark text
  • Clear visual states for verified vs. unverified profiles
  • Consistent badge and tag patterns across job cards and profiles
  • Legible typography across both desktop and mobile breakpoints

The goal was to make the product feel warm and community-driven, reflecting a female-founded brand, while staying clean and easy to scan.

The Insights

The biggest insight was that trust needed to be a search-result-level decision, not a profile-page-level one.

If users have to click into a profile to find out whether someone is verified, the platform has already lost the moment where trust actually matters.

Another insight was that the supply side needed its own kind of confidence-building, not just the demand side.

Female tradespeople needed a way to look credible fast, through portfolio-style profiles, since they don't have the years-long referral networks their male counterparts often rely on.

These insights shaped the design decisions:

  • Verification status shows directly in search results and job cards
  • Tradesperson profiles work like a mini portfolio, not just a listing
  • Reviews and job history are surfaced early, not buried
  • A shareable badge mechanic turns bookings into social proof
  • The visual language stays warm and community-driven, not corporate

These insights helped shape SheTrades into a product that supports both safety and discoverability at once.

The Solution

The solution is a two-sided web marketplace that makes trust visible at every step, from search to booking to review.

SheTrades allows users to:

Search and filter by trade View verified, female-only profiles Post a job and receive quotes Message and book safely in one place Share a digital badge of support

The verification and review system became the core of the product, it's what turns a directory into something people actually feel safe using.

The result is a platform where safety is a product feature, not a footnote, designed to work equally well on desktop and mobile since it's used in the moment a problem comes up at home.

The Results

The result is a marketplace that treats safety and visibility as core product features, not afterthoughts.

The design helps:

  • Make trust visible at the point of decision, not buried in a profile
  • Give underrepresented tradeswomen a credible way to be discovered
  • Turn a simple booking into a shareable, community-building moment
  • Work consistently across desktop and mobile, since home problems don't wait for the right device

It reframes booking a home service from a transactional task into something that feels safer and more human on both sides of the marketplace.

The Learnings

This project taught me how to design from a business strategy document rather than a finished brief.

Working from a Lean Canvas and a goals map meant translating founder-level thinking into concrete interface decisions myself, rather than working off a feature list.

1. What Went Well

Turning an abstract idea like a "digital badge for social proof" into a concrete, designed engagement mechanic, instead of leaving it as a marketing bullet point.

2. What Could Be Improved

With real users, I'd want to test how much verification detail people actually need before they trust a listing, and whether the badge mechanic drives repeat use, not just novelty.

3. What I Learned

This project sharpened my ability to separate business goals from user-facing design problems, and to design for two very different sides of a marketplace at once.
